# Chart Big Idea

Every chart needs a claim. The Big Idea is the sentence the chart should prove at a glance.

## When to use

Use before:

- creating a Flint chart
- writing a chart title or caption
- deciding between chart types
- turning a table into a figure
- reviewing whether a visual artifact communicates the intended point

## Output

Produce a short chart brief:

```text
Big Idea:
Audience:
Decision or use:
Measure and units:
Comparison:
Recommended chart type:
Risk to avoid:
```

## Method

1. **Name the reader's task.** What decision, scan, or explanation does the chart support?
2. **Find the comparison.** Time trend, category ranking, distribution, relationship, composition, flow, or target progress.
3. **State the claim.** Write one sentence that someone could argue with.
4. **Check the evidence.** Confirm the data can actually support the claim.
5. **Choose the stance.** Neutral, diagnostic, cautionary, comparative, or prescriptive.
6. **Compress.** Remove words until another removal would damage the claim.

## Title rules

Good chart titles state a finding:

| Weak | Strong |
| --- | --- |
| Revenue by quarter | Scout shows the fastest revenue climb by Q4 |
| Support tickets by hour | Midday is the busiest support window |
| Implementation duration | Most implementations finish inside four weeks |

Use subtitles for scope, population, time period, and units.

## Tests

A chart Big Idea is ready when:

- it names what changed, differs, clusters, leads, lags, or matters
- the chart's encodings directly support the claim
- the title still makes sense if the reader sees only the visual for three seconds
- it avoids overclaiming beyond the data

## Failure modes

- **Topic title:** names the metric but not the finding.
- **Unsupported claim:** title says "best" or "fastest" without data that proves it.
- **Decorative surprise:** chart type attracts attention but weakens the comparison.
- **Missing stakes:** the figure is true but irrelevant to the reader's decision.
